\section{Overview}

My capstone is now a study of how honest the uncertainty around a glucose forecast is. People with type 1 diabetes try to keep their glucose between two lines. Below 70 mg/dL is hypoglycemia, glucose that is too low, which can turn dangerous quickly. Above 180 mg/dL is hyperglycemia, glucose that is too high, which harms the body when it lasts. A forecast that looks 60 minutes ahead matters because an hour is enough time to act, for example by eating or by adjusting insulin, before the reading crosses either line.

A forecaster predicts glucose 60 minutes ahead, and a band around the forecast tells the person how far to trust it. An 80\% band is honest if the true value falls inside it 80\% of the time. The question I am answering is whether the band stays honest where it matters: in each situation (the current glucose level plus the direction it has moved over the last 30 minutes), for each patient, and on each day. I tested this on 215 patients from 11 public studies. Three studies were used to design the methods. The other eight (153 patients) were held out and used only to check them.

The project started as a replication of GLIMMER \parencite{glimmer2026}, a published study that weights a forecaster's mistakes in dangerous glucose ranges more heavily. The following sections explain how I got from there to here: the turns and dead ends, how I defined success, how I used generative AI, how I used feedback, and what remains before the Full Draft. The work product reports the results; this document is about how I reached them.

Two parts of the process are the ones I am proudest of. The first is catching my own errors. Several times a result looked like progress until I checked it again, and each time I treated the correction as part of the work rather than something to hide. The second is the deep understanding of the material I gained along the way. I started with little knowledge of diabetes data or of how uncertainty is measured, and I now understand where forecast errors come from, which ideas from finance, weather and statistics carry over to glucose and which do not, and why a band that looks fine on average can fail the people who need it most.

\subsection{Starting Point and the GLIMMER Replication}

At the start of the semester I was debating between projects that would support my career path and projects I found interesting. I concluded that I would rather work on something I enjoy and can happily pour my time into, so I chose this one.

The first step was finding data. The standard dataset, OhioT1DM \parencite{marling2020ohio}, requires an access request. I requested access, and the dataset's maintainer granted it about five days later, in mid-September. In the meantime I had started with AZT1D \parencite{azt1d2025}, a newer dataset of 25 patients. I also found OhioT1DM inside MetaboNet \parencite{metabonet2026}, a public collection of diabetes datasets, and I checked MetaboNet row by row against AZT1D's own files before relying on it.

I began by replicating GLIMMER \parencite{glimmer2026}, a 2026 study from the group that built AZT1D. It trains forecasters to weight mistakes in dangerous glucose ranges more heavily, and it tunes that weighting for each patient. In my replication, the weighted models caught more dangerous events (the share caught rose from 0.507 to 0.722), but overall accuracy was worse.

Then I found a bug: each patient's weight search had started from the same random seed, so every search was identical. After the fix, the gain from per-patient tuning disappeared (a typical error of 41.12 mg/dL for the tuned model against 41.18 mg/dL for fixed weights). This taught me to dive deeper into whatever I analyze, rather than trusting a result at first sight. Since then I re-check results before relying on them.

After my first presentation, Prof.\ Watson suggested forecasting the whole glucose curve step by step, which is called recursive forecasting. The model predicted the next 5 minutes, fed its own prediction back in as if it were real data, and repeated this to build the curve over the next several hours. To keep going on its own, the model had to predict every input together: insulin, meals and glucose. The curve came out as a nearly straight line, because the model always guessed that no meal was coming. Adding the time of day as a fixed input, since meals cluster at certain hours, did not help. When I gave the model the real logged meals instead of asking it to guess them, the curve improved and rose with the meal, but this was on one patient and only because the model was told the meals in advance. The approach could not predict meals, which is the part a person would need, so I set it aside. I wrote at the time that my next steps were unclear to me.

\subsection{From Forecasting to Uncertainty}

Building a better prediction model is a crowded space, with many groups competing on accuracy. I decided to expand into something more niche: the uncertainty around a forecast. I thought I had a better chance of finding a gap in the research there. So in late September I moved from improving the forecast to estimating how far to trust it.

The idea was to put a band around each forecast and use it as a warning. If the band's edge crossed into the dangerous range, meaning its lower edge fell below 70 mg/dL or its upper edge rose above 180 mg/dL, it triggered a warning (Figure~\ref{fig:alarm}). A wide band warns often, and a narrow band warns rarely.

To judge the forecasts and their warnings I used the Clarke error grid \parencite{clarke1987}, a standard chart that sorts glucose estimates by how much harm an error could cause (Figure~\ref{fig:clarke}). Each forecast is one point, with the actual glucose on one axis and the forecast on the other. Zone A is clinically accurate. Zone B is off but harmless. Zone C would lead to unnecessary treatment. Zone D is a dangerous miss: the person is actually low or high, but the forecast says normal. Zone E points the wrong way, calling a low a high or the reverse. Throughout this document, dangerous readings means real lows and highs, and zone D is what a warning system must avoid.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering

\caption{The Clarke error grid, with zones A to E labelled. Each dot is one 60-minute forecast for one AZT1D patient, placed by the actual glucose and the forecast.}
\label{fig:clarke}
\end{figure}

I borrowed band methods from other fields: GARCH \parencite{bollerslev1986garch}, which tracks changing volatility in financial markets; the analog ensemble \parencite{dellemonache2013analog}, which builds a weather forecast from similar past situations; and conformal prediction \parencite{vovk2005alrw}, a statistical method that sizes the band from past errors.

The bands failed my first test. Ordinary 80\% and 95\% bands raised alarms on 68\% to 100\% of safe readings. When I compared the methods at the same false-alarm rate, they caught between 74\% and 76.5\% of dangerous readings, so none was clearly better (Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff}). I then added three more methods: EWMA, another volatility tracker from finance; locally weighted conformal prediction, which sizes the band from errors on similar windows; and time-of-day bands, which use past errors at the same hour. This did not change the picture: all six alarm curves were nearly identical.

\begin{figure}[htbp]

\caption{The share of real dangerous readings that set off the alarm against the share of safe readings that set it off, for all danger, lows and highs. Every method lands on nearly the same curve.}
\label{fig:tradeoff}
\end{figure}

In late September I looked at what widening the band actually did on the Clarke grid (Figure~\ref{fig:garchzones}). Making the GARCH band wider pushed more forecasts into zone C, the false alarms, from about 0.1\% to 2.5\%, while zone D, the dangerous misses, barely dropped. A wider band mostly added alarms. I wrote in my journal: ``I don't think that I would be able to actually create meaningful insights without increasing the false hyper/hypoglycemic rates drastically.''

Instead of testing more methods, I then looked at where the error came from. More data would barely help, and the model did not degrade over time. What did stand out was that the band was honest on average but not in every situation, which pointed to the direction I took next.

\subsection{Choosing One Direction}

The direction I am pursuing is a situation-aware band. A situation is the current glucose level plus the direction it has moved over the last 30 minutes, for example ``between 70 and 180 mg/dL and falling fast.'' Each situation gets its own band width, learned from past errors in that situation. Statisticians call this kind of grouped calibration Mondrian conformal prediction \parencite{vovk2005alrw}.

It made the bands far more consistent across situations (Figure~\ref{fig:situation}). Pooled over 11 studies, the standard GARCH band's coverage ranged from 62\% to 91\% across the 25 situations, while the situation-aware band ranged from 72\% to 82\%, and mostly from 78\% to 82\%. It also held on data it was never designed on. On OhioT1DM, with the rule for success written down first, the distance from the 80\% target fell by 41\%, 9 of 12 patients were closer, and the bands were 12\% narrower. It did not catch more of the large errors, so the gain is in honesty, not in catching more danger.

\section{Outcome Analysis}

When I started in early September, I thought success would mean a better forecast, or better warnings before a low or a high. By late September, as described in Navigation, I doubted that this was possible, and the doubt held up: compared fairly, at the same rate of false alarms, every band I tried warned about as well as the others (Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff}). So I stopped trying to beat other methods on a single score and started trying to understand the data well enough to say something true and new about it. For this stage, success means analyzing the data in depth, coming away with a clearer understanding of it, and producing findings that can be compiled into a scientific paper and submitted to a conference.

\section{Use of Generative AI}

Claude Code, an AI coding assistant made by Anthropic, writes most of my code. I decide what to test and check what comes back. That split has held since my first week, when I wrote in my journal that I was working ``more as a validator than anything else'' while I replicated GLIMMER. What changed over the semester is how much the assistant does on its own. Until mid-October, it asked me before any run longer than 10 minutes. Then I wrote a research protocol and let the assistant work under it with no check-ins. The protocol is what makes that safe, so I describe it first.

\subsection{The Research Protocol}

The protocol, saved in the project repository, turns every idea into a test with the same steps.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Every idea becomes a hypothesis.} It is written as one sentence about something that can be measured. ``Does the patient explain more of the forecast error than the situation?'' qualifies. ``What drives uncertainty?'' does not.
\item \textbf{The rule for success is written down and saved before any test runs.} The hypothesis goes into the register with the measure, the threshold that counts as success and the data it will use. The entry is saved in version control (git), which time-stamps every change, so anyone can check that the rule came before the result. The rule cannot move after the run. If I think of a better design after a failure, it becomes a new hypothesis, labelled as a second or third try.
\item \textbf{The AI runs the smallest test that can answer it.} It reuses saved models and results wherever it can and trains only what the question needs.
\item \textbf{The result is judged only against the saved rule.} The verdict is supported, not supported or inconclusive. Anything noticed afterwards is labelled as noticed afterwards.
\item \textbf{Failed ideas stay in the register.} Nothing is deleted. A line of work stops after two failures in a row that suggest nothing new to test, and a method is never tuned until it wins. If it takes many tries, the number of tries is reported.
\item \textbf{A finding must hold on studies never used to build the method before it counts.} A result on one dataset is a lead. A result that holds on two or more datasets, and still holds when patients are resampled, is a finding. Only a result that also holds on the eight outside studies can be a main result of the paper.
\item \textbf{Everything is logged.} Each unit of work gets an entry in a work log with links to the code and result files behind it, and each week has a short summary of what was found and what is still open.
\end{enumerate}

The protocol also carries the statistical rules I had learned the hard way: the patient, not the reading, is the unit of analysis; test data stays out of fitting and calibration; every model and patient gets its own random seed; and methods are compared at a matched width or a matched false-alarm rate, never by which one is wider. Since then the assistant has run dozens of tests this way. Writing the rules down is what made it safe to let it work without asking me each time.

\section{Feedback and How I Used It}

Prof.\ Watson's written feedback on my early proposal made three research suggestions. I treated each one as a question to test, with a rule for success written down first. Below, each suggestion is followed by what I did and what it showed.

\subsection{Approximate Entropy to Model Major Shifts}

\textit{The suggestion.} Prof.\ Watson suggested exploring approximate entropy \parencite{pincus1991apen}, a measure of how irregular a series is, to model the major shifts in a patient's glucose.

\textit{What I did.} In early October I tested sample entropy \parencite{richman2000sampen}, a close relative of approximate entropy that is less sensitive to the length of the record. I asked whether a patient's sample entropy, measured on the first part of their data, could predict how large their forecast errors would be later, beyond what the plain spread of their glucose already predicts. Before running, I set the rule that the measure had to cut the prediction error by at least 15\%.

\textit{What it showed.} Sample entropy added only a little: 9\% beyond how variable a patient's glucose is, short of the 15\% I had set. This was on the 25 AZT1D patients with one training seed, so it is preliminary. I recorded it as a hint, not a finding, and did not build on it.

\subsection{Autoencoders}

\textit{The suggestion.} Prof.\ Watson also suggested autoencoders, networks that compress an input into a few numbers and then rebuild it, so that the few numbers act as a summary.

\textit{What I did.} I trained an autoencoder that compresses two hours of readings into a short summary, first on AZT1D and then on HUPA-UCM \parencite{hupa2024}. I first drew the summaries with PCA, a method that keeps the directions along which the data varies most. Then, at Prof.\ Watson's suggestion, I drew them with t-SNE instead, a method that places similar points close together on a flat map and so shows groups that PCA can hide. I also tried sizing the bands by groups learned from the summaries instead of by my hand-drawn situation grid.

\textit{What it showed.} The autoencoders mostly captured glucose level and direction. With PCA, the main direction of the summary tracked current glucose almost exactly. The t-SNE maps sorted the windows by current level and then by trend, the same structure as the situation grid I was already using (Figure~\ref{fig:tsne}). Bands built from the learned groups did no better than the hand-drawn grid. Because the autoencoder showed nothing beyond level and trend, I closed this line in early October. When I returned to it in mid-October with a stricter test, level and trend explained 87\% of what the autoencoder kept, which confirmed that decision.

\subsection{Separating Meals and Insulin From Changes That Are Not Externally Predictable}

\textit{The suggestion.} Prof.\ Watson noted that most major glucose changes come from outside factors such as eating and insulin, which should be filtered out, and that subtler features producing major changes, ones that are not externally predictable, would be really good to know about.

\textit{What I did.} I took the part of the autoencoder summary that level and trend did not explain and asked which recorded variables explained it for patients the model had not seen. The candidates included carbohydrates, insulin doses, insulin still active in the body, basal insulin, heart rate, steps and time of day. Later, across all 215 patients from 11 studies, I asked what makes some patients harder to forecast than others.

\textit{What it showed.} Meals and insulin explained almost none of what was left. The leftover tracked mainly the patient and their basal insulin setting, and it did not help predict how wrong a forecast would be. The subtler driver turned out to be how fast a patient's glucose moves. The spread of each patient's 30-minute glucose changes explained 59\% of the differences in forecast difficulty in studies left out of the fit, against 44\% for the overall spread of their glucose. It also accounted for children and closed-loop users being harder to forecast: once it was included, neither group differed clearly from the others. This answer came from following his third suggestion, and it is now one of the findings in the paper.

\subsection{Major Learning Outcomes}

\begin{description}
\item[\#cs156-MLCode] The project rests on several machine learning systems written in Python: per-patient CNN-LSTM forecasters that read recent readings and predict glucose an hour ahead, autoencoders, deep ensembles, and a quantile network that predicts its own band. The AI wrote most of this code, and I read all of it, as described in Use of Generative AI. Reading it taught me that working is a weak standard. The seed bug in the GLIMMER replication ran without a single error and produced a believable gain, yet every per-patient search was identical (Navigation). Working code can still be wrong, so every model and every patient now gets its own seed by rule. Performance mattered too. Training one forecaster per patient across many studies stayed manageable because saved models and results are reused rather than retrained for each new question, and long training jobs can resume where they stopped.

\item[\#cs156-MLExplaination] I had to explain what the models learned, not only how well they scored. For the autoencoder, I drew its summaries with PCA and then with t-SNE, and the t-SNE map showed in one picture that it had sorted the windows by glucose level and then by trend (Figure~\ref{fig:tsne}). That picture is what let me close the line with confidence. In writing, this document introduces each band method in one plain clause, such as GARCH as a volatility tracker from finance and conformal prediction as a way to size the band from past errors, and it describes an honest band as one that contains the true value as often as it claims. Orally, I explained the project in slides for my second-reader meeting in early October, including my doubt about whether the direction was valuable.

\item[\#cs166-EmpiricalAnalysis] My main simulation used patients from simglucose, an open-source type 1 diabetes simulator, with sensor noise of a known size planted on top. Because the planted noise was known, it could act as an answer key: a good band should widen by about as much noise as I added. The results taught me two things. The answer key itself was too simple: noisy inputs also made the forecasts worse, so the error grew by more than the noise I planted. And the simulated patients were far easier to forecast than real ones, so the simulation could not answer my question (Navigation). I also used simulation in a second way, re-creating the data as if my idea were false. I shuffled the situation labels, and later shifted them in time within each patient, many times, to see how good or uneven results look by chance alone. A result counted only if it stood clear of that chance picture.

\item[\#cs114-ModelSelection] Several of my questions came down to which explanation best fits the data. When forecast errors varied, I asked whether the situation, the patient or the day accounted for more of it, and compared these accounts directly rather than assuming one. The situation explained more of how large an error would be, while most of the unevenness left in the bands was between patients, which is why the final band has one adjustment for each (Outcome Analysis). When comparing band methods, I compared them at a matched false-alarm rate, because a wider band always looks safer (Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff}). Before calling any unevenness real, I compared it with a chance-only model, the unevenness that sampling alone produces. This changed my reading more than once: a method that looked more even was mostly less noisy, not better calibrated, and some unevenness that looked like a failure was no larger than chance.

\item[\#cs110-CodeReadability] I organized the code so that someone else could read and run it. Shared code lives in one library that stays in place, while each week's scripts and notebooks sit in that week's folder, which separates reused code from code for one experiment. Project rules fix how every script and notebook finds the repository root, so no path breaks when a file is copied into a new week. When I moved the notebooks into weekly folders, I checked that each one still found its files from its new place. Conventions for names, comments and file placement are written in the instructions the AI reads at the start of each session, and the data loaders stop with a message that says what is missing rather than failing silently. Everything except the raw data, which is not ours to share, is in a public GitHub repository, and the dataset catalog tells a reader where to get each dataset.
\end{description}

\subsection{Habits of Mind}

\begin{description}
\item[\#hypothesisdevelopment] Most of my hypotheses grew from an observation. In late September the bands were honest on average but not in every situation: the same 80\% band covered too little in some situations and too much in others (Figure~\ref{fig:situation}). The mechanism I proposed was that how hard glucose is to predict depends on where it is and where it is heading, so one band width is too narrow in some situations and too wide in others. GARCH, borrowed from finance, rests on a related idea: the size of errors changes with conditions. I also wrote down the main alternative, that the patient rather than the situation explains how large the errors are; the situation explained more on most datasets. Later, when some patients proved harder to forecast, I looked for a mechanism rather than a label. If a patient's glucose moves faster, more can change within an hour, so their errors are larger. That one mechanism also explained why children and closed-loop users seemed harder to forecast, against the alternative that the groups themselves were the cause.
\item[\#testability] I tried to make each hypothesis produce several predictions, chosen so that a competing explanation would predict something different. For the situation-aware band, the first prediction was that it would be more even across situations than GARCH. That alone could not rule out a duller explanation: splitting the data into many groups helps, whatever the groups are. So a second test shuffled the situation labels and predicted that the real situations would beat every shuffled version. A third predicted that the gain would hold for a second forecasting model, and a fourth that it would hold on studies never used to design the method. Each had its own rule, set before the run, as described in Use of Generative AI. I also asked whether a test was practical, not only possible in principle. The simulated patients failed that check, because they were too easy to forecast to be informative. Prof.\ Watson's idea about subtler drivers of glucose change was hard to test as stated, so I turned it into a narrower, measurable question.
\item[\#gapanalysis] I first asked what already existed. Accuracy-focused forecasting is crowded, so a slightly better forecaster was unlikely to be my contribution. For bands, I started from existing methods rather than from scratch: GARCH from finance, the analog ensemble from weather and conformal prediction from statistics. Each is well tested in its own field, but at a matched false-alarm rate they performed almost identically, and none made better alarms than a tuned threshold on the forecast itself. When I built the situation-aware band, a literature check showed that it already had a name, Mondrian conformal prediction \parencite{vovk2005alrw}, and I compared it with strong alternatives, deep ensembles \parencite{lakshminarayanan2017ensembles} and conformalized quantile regression \parencite{romano2019cqr}. The methods needed adapting, not reinventing. The gap I found is in how bands are judged and used: in my searches I found no glucose forecasting study that checks whether bands stay honest by situation, by patient and by day. The closest, an intensive-care study, reported coverage by glucose range. I record this as not found, not as proof that none exists.
\item[\#studyreplication] I began by replicating GLIMMER \parencite{glimmer2026}, a recent study from the group that built AZT1D, before trying to extend it. This was a partial replication. I kept its core design: one CNN-LSTM per patient, a loss that weights errors in dangerous ranges more heavily, and a per-patient search for those weights. I changed two things to test how far its results generalize: I added a second model type, a Transformer, and a second dataset, OhioT1DM \parencite{marling2020ohio}, with different patients. The main effect replicated on both datasets: the weighted models caught more dangerous events at the cost of overall accuracy. The per-patient gain did not survive once each search had its own seed, as described in Navigation. A failed replication can mean the original claim is wrong, my methods are weaker, or my version was not faithful enough, and I cannot yet tell which. It does mean the personalization claim should not be relied on without further replication. That lesson shaped the research program: a result counts only once it holds on studies never used to build the method.
\item[\#dataviz] I tried to match each plot to the question it answers. To show what a forecast error means clinically, I used the Clarke error grid, a scatter of forecast against actual glucose with zones drawn by clinical harm (Figure~\ref{fig:clarke}). To ask what widening a band does, I plotted the share of forecasts in each zone as the band widens (Figure~\ref{fig:garchzones}), which showed that wider bands mostly add false alarms. Coverage across situations is one value for each pair of level and trend, so I used a heat map with a color scale centered on 80\%, where cells that are too narrow and too wide stand out in opposite colors (Figure~\ref{fig:situation}). For the autoencoder I switched from PCA to t-SNE at Prof.\ Watson's suggestion and colored the same map by level and by trend (Figure~\ref{fig:tsne}). Because t-SNE distorts distances, I read it only as a check on grouping, not as a measurement. I wrote captions that state the finding, such as ``every method lands on nearly the same curve'' (Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff}), and I read each figure before writing up a result.
\item[\#confidenceintervals] Two kinds of interval run through this work. The first are the bands. An 80\% band is a prediction interval for one future reading rather than a confidence interval for a fixed value, but its claim works the same way: it is about the procedure. Across many forecasts, 80\% of such bands should contain the true value, so I check coverage on held-out data by situation and by patient rather than trusting the stated level. The second are confidence intervals for my results. Readings from one patient are not independent, so I build these by resampling whole patients or whole studies, because resampling single readings would make them too narrow. For example, children were harder to forecast than adults after accounting for glucose variability, with a 90\% interval of 3\% to 12\% higher error. The correct reading is that intervals built this way would contain the true difference in 90\% of repeated samples, not that there is a 90\% chance this one does. It excludes zero, yet the difference faded once I accounted for how fast glucose moves.
\item[\#sampling] The population I care about is people with type 1 diabetes who wear a glucose sensor, and my unit of analysis is the patient, not the reading. One patient contributes thousands of closely related readings, so treating each reading as independent would be pseudoreplication and would overstate the evidence. This matters in practice: in HUPA-UCM one patient makes up over half of the readings, so a pooled result there could describe one person. I therefore report results patient by patient and resample patients when estimating uncertainty. For representativeness, I chose outside studies that differ in age, insulin delivery and sensor: children and adults, closed-loop and other pumps, and three sensor brands. Within each, patients were drawn in random order from those with enough weeks of data, and those studies were held out from design. The sample still has limits. Everyone in it joined a research study, so it may not reflect all people with type 1 diabetes, and results from one seed or a few patients are labelled preliminary.
\item[\#biasidentification] The biases I had to watch most were my own. After weeks invested in uncertainty bands, I wanted them to work. That is the sunk cost effect: past effort makes it harder to accept that a path is not paying off. It feeds confirmation bias, the tendency to seek and accept evidence for what I already hope. When the bands failed my first test in late September, the two together made it tempting to hunt for a measure on which they looked good. The seed bug showed a second interaction. The tuned models seemed to lower the error, which matched what the published study led me to expect. The code ran without errors, and I leaned on a shortcut: working code is correct code. Because the result agreed with my expectation, nothing prompted me to question the shortcut. The planning fallacy shows in my timeline. I planned to finish this documentation by mid-October, and it slipped, which fits the pattern of judging a task by its best case rather than by how long similar work took before.
\item[\#biasmitigation] I cannot remove these biases, only make them harder to act on. Against confirmation bias, I write the rule for success before each test runs, as described in Use of Generative AI. A fixed bar leaves no room to pick a friendlier measure afterwards. Comparing methods at a matched false-alarm rate works the same way: a wider band cannot look better just by warning more. Against the working-code shortcut, I read all the code the assistant writes and re-check a result before relying on it, most of all when it looks good, since good news is what I least want to question. Against the sunk cost effect, I decided early in October that failed experiments are as much part of the capstone as successes, and I keep every one on record. A failure then counts as a result rather than wasted effort, which weakens the pull to keep going on a path that is not paying off. Against the planning fallacy, my plan-versus-actual table gives me a record of how long work really took. Bias can still enter through which questions I choose to ask.
\item[\#organization] I ordered the content so that each idea comes after what it depends on. The Overview gives the roadmap: the question, why it matters to a person with diabetes, and what the later sections cover. Terms such as hypoglycemia, the band and the Clarke grid are defined when they first appear, before any result uses them. Navigation is ordered by the turns in the project, with subsections named mostly for changes of direction, so a reader follows why the work moved and not only when. In Feedback, each suggestion has the same three parts, the suggestion, what I did and what it showed, so the entries can be compared. Every figure and table is referenced in the text where it supports a point. I considered two other structures. A strict chronological diary would match my journal but bury the decisions in daily detail. A section per hypothesis would be complete but lose the story that connects them. The paper is organized differently, around its findings, with each results heading stating what was found.
\item[\#professionalism] I followed the established conventions of academic work. This document is written in LaTeX in APA 7 style, with in-text citations and a reference list, within the structure the Midterm instructions set. I checked each reference against its source, which showed that I had credited MetaboNet to the wrong authors, and I corrected it. For data, I followed each source's access process. OhioT1DM required an access request, and I followed the form its maintainer sent before using it.
